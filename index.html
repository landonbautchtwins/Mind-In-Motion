🧠 Neural communication

A neuron's membrane potential changes as inputs arrive. If the axon hillock reaches threshold, an all-or-none action potential travels down the axon. At a chemical synapse, neurotransmitter release can excite or inhibit the next cell.

🩸 Endocrine signaling

Hormones are released by endocrine glands into the bloodstream and affect target cells with matching receptors. Hormonal effects often develop more slowly and last longer than neural signals. Stress responses can involve adrenaline and cortisol on different timescales.

🧬 Heredity + environment

Genes contribute to biological tendencies, but do not dictate a fixed behavior. Environmental exposures can influence gene regulation through mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modification. Effects depend on tissue, timing, and context; the game’s index is only a teaching metaphor, not a prediction of actual epigenetic change.

How to play

  1. Form a team of 2–5 and choose a discussion leader, scorekeeper, and biology explainer.
  2. Start the game. Read the scenario and chance card out loud.
  3. Discuss the three choices, then select the one your team agrees on.
  4. Review the biological feedback: neural threshold, simulated stress/hormone response, and gene-expression index.
  5. Earn the choice’s base points, +1 if neural input reaches the threshold, and +1 if stress ends at 4 or lower.
  6. Play all ten rounds. Use the debrief questions to connect inherited variation, environment, and behavior.

The simulation uses teaching values to make cause-and-effect visible. It is not a quantitative physiological model, and a lower stress score is not always biologically “better”—appropriate arousal can help performance.

Debrief questions

  • Which response showed fast neural signaling versus slower hormone signaling?
  • How did the environment change your team's response?
  • Why does an epigenetic effect not mean the DNA sequence changed?
  • Why can’t one game outcome predict a real person's behavior?
